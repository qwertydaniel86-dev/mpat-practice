Tips & Tricks for speed aptitude tests

Practical study notes written for this practice app. Not affiliated with, or endorsed by, the PAF, AFP or any other armed force; no real test items are reproduced. Always follow the official instructions given on test day.

1. What pilot aptitude batteries measure

Military pilot screening around the world tests broadly similar abilities under time pressure. The PAF's Military Pilot Aptitude Test (MPAT) is reported to cover spatial awareness, reasoning, mathematics, mechanical/aviation knowledge and mental speed, and it comes after the AFP Service Aptitude Test (AFPSAT) and PAF General Classification Test for officer applicants, alongside psychological/neuropsychiatric, medical and interview screening. The US Air Force's AFOQT has timed subtests such as Word Knowledge, Arithmetic Reasoning, Math Knowledge, Reading Comprehension, Instrument Comprehension, Block Counting, Table Reading and Aviation Information, and pilot candidates also take the computer-based TBAS (psychomotor, spatial and multitasking). The RAF's Defence Aptitude Assessment (formerly the Airman Selection Test) has six timed parts, and officer/aircrew candidates are assessed further at the Officer and Aircrew Selection Centre (OASC). Canada's CFAT measures verbal skills, spatial ability and problem solving; Australia's ADF starts with a 20-minute online Job Opportunities Assessment of numerical, verbal and abstract reasoning; Germany's DLR in Hamburg runs computer-based tests of basic cognitive abilities, operational competencies and personality for pilot applicants. Common thread: speed + accuracy on many short items, so technique matters as much as knowledge.

2. Time budgeting

  • Divide the time by the number of items before you start (e.g., 50 items in 10 min = 12 s each). Check the clock at the quarter marks, not after every item.
  • Never spend more than about twice the average time on one item. Mark it, guess if wrong answers are not penalised, and move on.
  • Practise here with the 5-minute limit to build pace, then 10 minutes for accuracy.

3. Skip-and-return & elimination

  • First pass: answer everything you can do in one look. Second pass: the "maybe" items. Last: the hard ones.
  • Cross out choices that are clearly impossible (wrong units, wrong sign, wrong size). Two eliminations turn a 25% guess into a 50% guess.
  • Find out beforehand whether wrong answers are penalised; if not, never leave a blank.

4. Estimation & mental-math shortcuts

  • Units digit first: in vertical addition/multiplication, the units digit of the answer often eliminates three choices immediately.
  • Round and compare: 48 + 73 + 29 ≈ 50 + 70 + 30 = 150, so look for an answer near 150.
  • Multiply 2-digit numbers by splitting: 47 × 23 = 47 × 20 + 47 × 3 = 940 + 141 = 1081. ×11: 63 × 11 = 6 (6+3) 3 = 693. ×5 = ×10 ÷ 2. ×25 = ×100 ÷ 4.
  • Subtraction by adding up: 92 − 57 → 57 + 3 = 60, + 32 = 92, so 35.
  • Casting out nines to check a sum: digit-sum of the answer must equal digit-sum of the parts (mod 9).

5. Word problems: recognise the structure

  • D = R × T. Toward each other → ADD speeds; same direction / catching up → SUBTRACT speeds.
  • Trains: passing a pole = own length; passing a platform/bridge = train + platform; two trains = both lengths ÷ (sum or difference of speeds).
  • Work & pipes: think "fraction of the job per hour". Rates add; drains/leaks are negative. Two workers: ab/(a+b). Fill + drain: ab/(b−a).
  • Ages: one variable for the present age; the age difference never changes.
  • Buildings & shadows: same sun → height/shadow ratios equal. 45° → rise = run. Use 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17 right triangles.
  • Expression answers: don't compute — set up the formula with the letters, then check the units (km ÷ km/h = hours). Plug in easy numbers (x = 2, y = 3) to test a choice.
  • Letter-diagram answers: find the start position, add/subtract the distance moved (speed × time), then read which labelled point you land on.

6. Inspection / defect rows: pattern scanning

  • Study the first object of each row for 1–2 seconds and name its parts ("handle, clip, tip").
  • Sweep left to right checking ONE feature at a time (all tips, then all clips). The eye spots a missing part faster than a "different" whole object.
  • Expect at most one defect per row here, and some perfect rows: once you find it, jump to the next row.
  • Keep a steady rhythm; don't re-check finished rows unless you have spare time.

7. Mental rotation: dice & clocks

  • Dice: faces that ever appear together in a view are neighbours, never opposite. Collect each face's neighbours; the one face left over is opposite. On a cube each face has exactly four neighbours.
  • For "which cube could it be", reject any picture that puts two opposite faces side by side, then check the order (clockwise) of the three faces around the shared corner — a mirror order is impossible.
  • Clocks: read the arrow sweep in eighths of a circle (45° each). Follow one landmark only — usually the 12 — and ask where it will be after the turn: 90° clockwise puts 12 at the right, 180° at the bottom, 90° counter-clockwise at the left. Combine two arrows by adding clockwise and subtracting counter-clockwise turns.
  • Rotate the picture, not yourself: keep your head still and imagine the paper turning.

8. Shape completion / odd fit

  • Count squares first: a piece with the wrong number of squares can never fit.
  • Then compare the outline: look for the "corner" or "bump" of the gap and find it on each piece.
  • Pieces may be turned but not flipped — a mirror-image piece does NOT fit.

9. Reading: skim strategies

  • Read the questions first, then skim the passage: first and last sentences of each paragraph carry the main idea.
  • "Main idea" and "best title" answers cover the WHOLE passage, not one detail. Answers with "always", "never", "only" are usually wrong.
  • For vocabulary-in-context, replace the word with each choice and reread the sentence.
  • Answer only from the passage, not from outside knowledge.

10. Vocabulary: roots, prefixes, suffixes

  • Prefixes: bene- good (benevolent), mal- bad (malevolent), in-/im-/un- not (immutable), circum- around (circumspect), pre- before, re- again/back (refute), de- down/away (deride), ex- out (exonerate), omni- all (omnipresent).
  • Roots: loqu speak (loquacious), ver true (veracity), cred believe (credulous), vor eat (voracious), mut change (immutable), pac/plac peace (placate), culp guilt (culpable), magn great, anim mind/spirit (equanimity).
  • Suffixes: -ous/-ious full of (copious), -ity quality of (paucity), -ate to make (alleviate), -ible/-able can be (feasible).
  • Decide if the word "feels" positive or negative; that alone often eliminates two choices. Many distractors are antonyms.

11. Mechanical principles cheat sheet

  • Levers: effort × effort arm = load × load arm. 1st class: fulcrum in middle (seesaw); 2nd: load in middle (wheelbarrow); 3rd: effort in middle (tweezers).
  • Pulleys: ideal MA = number of rope segments holding the moving block. Inclined plane: longer ramp → less force, more distance.
  • Gears: meshed gears turn opposite ways; an idler restores direction; small gear driving big gear → slower, more torque. Speed ratio = teeth ratio.
  • Fluids: Pascal — pressure in a closed fluid is transmitted equally (hydraulic brakes, jacks). Narrow pipe → faster flow, lower pressure (Bernoulli).
  • Engines: four-stroke = intake, compression, power, exhaust (2 crank revolutions). Two-stroke = power every revolution; mixture goes intake → crankcase → transfer port → cylinder; piston opens the ports; oil is mixed with fuel.
  • Brakes: pedal → master cylinder → brake lines/fluid → calipers squeeze pads on the rotor (disc) or wheel cylinders push shoes against the drum. Spongy pedal = air in lines. ABS pulses pressure so wheels don't lock and you can still steer.

12. Test-day preparation

  • Protect sleep for the whole week before, not just the last night; no cramming new material the night before.
  • Eat a normal meal, bring the required documents, IDs and pencils, arrive early, and use the restroom before the start.
  • Listen to every instruction: timing, how to mark answers and whether guessing is penalised differ between tests.

13. Managing anxiety

  • Box breathing: in 4 s, hold 4 s, out 4 s, hold 4 s — two rounds before each section.
  • Expect to not finish every section; speed tests are designed that way. One hard item is worth the same as an easy one.
  • If you blank, drop that item, answer the next easy one to regain rhythm, then come back.
  • Talk to yourself like an instructor: "next item, eyes on the question".
